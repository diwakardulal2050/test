1. Executive Summary
ICC-IMS occupies a unique position in the pavement management industry. Born from the 2022
merger of International Cybernetics Company (ICC) and Infrastructure Management Services
(IMS), the combined entity controls the full lifecycle of pavement intelligence: from the design,
manufacture, and integration of precision data collection equipment, to field data collection and
engineering consulting services, to proprietary software platforms for pavement management
analysis, visualization, and stakeholder communication. This end-to-end capability is genuinely
rare in the industry and represents a significant competitive advantage.

However, the current website at icc-ims.com does not translate this advantage into a clear,
compelling digital experience for the three primary buyer segments that drive ICC-IMS revenue:

    •   State Departments of Transportation (DOTs) purchasing high-accuracy profiling
        equipment, friction testers, and network-level data collection systems for state highway
        programs.
    •   Municipalities and Counties contracting for pavement condition surveys, management
        plans, budget scenario analysis, council presentations, and ongoing SaaS-based
        pavement management through ESA platforms.
    •   AEC Firms (Kimley-Horn, Tetra Tech, Mott MacDonald, AtkinsRealis) procuring
        subcontract pavement data collection services, airfield assessment capabilities, and
        technical deliverables for integration into their own project workflows.


Each of these audiences arrives at icc-ims.com with fundamentally different questions,
different technical vocabularies, different procurement constraints, and different
definitions of success. The current site architecture is organized around what ICC-IMS does
and sells rather than around the problems each buyer segment is trying to solve. This
organizational mismatch forces visitors to work too hard to locate the content relevant to them,
increasing bounce rates and reducing the likelihood of conversion.


  Core Finding
  The ICC-IMS website has a seller-centric architecture in a market that demands buyer-centric
  navigation. The site contains strong content and legitimate proof points, but they are fragmented

2. Methodology and Site Audit
This analysis is based on a comprehensive structural and content review of icc-ims.com
conducted in March 2026, encompassing the homepage, all Markets sub-pages (DOTs,
Counties and Municipalities, Civil Engineering Firms, Concessionaires, Airports, Federal
Agencies), the full Services section organized by customer type, all Equipment pages (Profilers,
Pavement Survey, Friction Testers, Reference Devices), all Software pages (Unify suite
including Drive, Connect, and Inform; ESA; ESWA; Your Software), the Projects/Case Studies
gallery, the Consulting Team page, Support, Company pages, and the Cooperative Purchasing
section. The review was conducted against best practices in B2B technical website UX, content
journey mapping for government and AEC buyer personas, and current SEO/GEO optimization
standards.

2.2 Critical Findings from Structural Audit


  Critical Finding #1: Parallel Navigation Creates Decision Paralysis
  The site maintains two parallel but disconnected organizational structures: ‘Markets’ (who they serve)
  and ‘Services’ (what they offer, re-organized by customer type). These sections contain overlapping
  content and provide no clear signal to a first-time visitor about which path is correct for them. A DOT
  engineer looking for profiling equipment must choose between Markets > DOTs, Equipment >
  Profilers, or Services > State/Provincial - three different entry points with no indication which will
  answer their question fastest.



  Critical Finding #2: The Homepage Does Not Route Visitors
  The homepage headline (‘Leading the Way in Data-Driven Pavement & Sidewalk Management’) is
  generic and fails to differentiate ICC-IMS for any specific buyer persona. There is no mechanism for
  a visitor to self-identify (‘I am a DOT engineer’ / ‘I am a city public works director’ / ‘I am an AEC
  project manager’) and be directed to content relevant to their specific needs. The testimonials are
  strong but not organized by segment.



  Critical Finding #3: Three Pillars, Zero Connective Narrative
  Equipment, Services, and Software are siloed in separate navigation trees with no cross-linking and
  no ‘complete solution’ narrative connecting them. One of ICC-IMS’s most powerful value proposition -
  that you have intense deep knowledge and control of the full data lifecycle from collection hardware
  through processing to management and visualization - is never articulated in a single, compelling
  flow anywhere on the site.

        between a 6-month RFP process and a 2-week contract execution) but is positioned at
        the far right of the navigation with no promotional emphasis on key audience pages.
        Most municipal and DOT visitors will never find it organically.

    •   Software Naming Confusion: ‘Your Software’ and ‘Our Software’ labels create
        unnecessary ambiguity. A first-time visitor does not immediately understand what this
        distinction means. The Unify suite naming convention (Drive, Connect, Inform) is opaque
        without context, and the relationship between Unify, ESA, and ESWA is unclear.
    •   Projects Section Architecture: Case studies are presented as a geographic gallery
        (Arizona DOT, Dallas TX, Jacksonville FL, etc.) rather than as an outcome-focused proof
        library. There is no ability to filter by customer type, service type, or project outcome,
        making it difficult for a DOT buyer to quickly find DOT-relevant case studies or for an
        AEC firm to find subcontract project examples.
    •   Blog Isolation: Blog content is buried under Company > Blogs rather than integrated
        into service or market pages. This severs the content marketing value chain: blog posts
        that could drive organic traffic to service pages are instead isolated islands with no
        internal linking strategy and challenging SEO.
    •   Missing Conversion Assets: There are no downloadable spec sheets for any
        equipment model, no sample pavement management reports, no ROI calculators, no
        budget scenario planning tool previews, no ‘What Do I Need?’ self-assessment quiz, and
        no downloadable capability statement for AEC subcontractor pre-qualification. These are
        standard conversion assets in B2B technical marketing and their absence represents
        significant lost conversion opportunity. Integrating even some of these would provide
        additional engagement.
    •   Equipment Pages Lack Standards Context: While equipment pages describe features
        and capabilities well, they do not systematically reference the ASTM, AASHTO, and
        FHWA standards that DOT and AEC buyers use as their primary evaluation criteria. For

        certification, but this information appears deep in the page content rather than
        prominently in specifications tables. These are searchable terms for organic traffic.
    •   Mobile Navigation Complexity: The Services mega-menu with three customer-type
        columns and 12+ items per column creates a particularly challenging mobile experience.
        Government and AEC buyers frequently research vendors on mobile devices during
        conferences and field visits.
    •   Inconsistent CTAs: The primary conversion actions (‘Book a Discovery Call,’ ‘Request
        a Quote,’ ‘Contact Us’) are inconsistently placed across the site. Several key pages have
        no CTA at all, and the existing CTAs do not differentiate between buyer types or buying
        stages.

4. Journey Maps by Customer Segment
The following journey maps trace each primary persona’s experience from initial awareness
through post-engagement, identifying specific friction points and opportunities at each stage of
interaction with the ICC-IMS website. Each journey map identifies the visitor’s mindset, typical
entry path, current site experience, friction points encountered, and specific opportunities to
improve the experience and increase conversion likelihood.

4.1 Persona A - State DOT Engineer Journey
Stage 1: Awareness and Entry
Mindset: The DOT engineer is typically in research or specification or RFP-development mode.
They may be evaluating profiler manufacturers for a state equipment purchase, researching
friction testing options for a highway safety program, or looking for a data collection service
provider for a specific corridor assessment.

Typical Entry: Google search for terms like ‘pavement profiler equipment AASHTO compliant,’
‘IRI measurement vehicle state DOT,’ ‘locked wheel friction tester ASTM E274,’ ‘network-level
pavement data collection services,’ or direct brand searches for ‘ICC profiler’ or ‘IrisPRO.’ They
may also arrive via colleague referral or from a conference/trade show interaction.

Current Experience: Landing on the homepage, they see the headline ‘Leading the Way in
Data-Driven Pavement & Sidewalk Management.’ The word ‘sidewalk’ is irrelevant to a state
highway engineer and immediately introduces doubt about whether this company serves their
market. The merger story and testimonials from municipal clients further reinforce the
perception that ICC-IMS may be primarily a local agency services company.


  Friction Point
  The homepage provides no immediate signal that ICC-IMS is a serious equipment manufacturer
  serving state DOTs. The ‘sidewalk management’ emphasis in the headline and the municipal
  testimonials create a misalignment with the DOT engineer’s expectations. There is no ‘DOT
  Solutions’ or ‘Equipment’ CTA in the hero section, and no segment-routing mechanism to help this
  visitor self-identify and reach relevant content quickly. There is little to no mention of industry
  standards, of which are great interest to DOTs.

Stage 2: Consideration and Evaluation
Current Experience: The DOT engineer navigates to either Markets > DOTs or Equipment >
Profilers. They are evaluating whether ICC-IMS equipment meets state and federal standards
and comparing it against competitors. The Equipment section has strong product pages with
detailed descriptions of the IrisPRO Every Speed, IrisPRO High Speed, IrisPRO Lightweight,
IrisPRO Pave, and friction testing equipment. The IrisPRO Every Speed page, for instance,
describes the patented ESP technology for accurate profiling at any speed including stop-and-
go conditions - a genuinely differentiating capability.

Key Strengths Found: The equipment pages describe the IrisPRO Pave’s LCMS-2 integration,
3D pavement scanning at speeds up to 75 mph, sub-millimeter crack detection, and ASTM
Class 1 inertial profiler capabilities. The IrisPRO Every Speed’s patented variable-speed
profiling eliminates the traditional 12 mph minimum speed constraint. These are powerful
differentiators that competitors cannot match.

    •   Missing Standards Compliance Matrix: While individual product pages mention ASTM
        E950, AASHTO R56, and TxDOT certifications in body text, there is no consolidated
        standards compliance table showing which equipment models meet which standards.
        DOT engineers evaluate equipment against specific standard requirements, and a matrix
        format would dramatically accelerate their evaluation process.
    •   No Downloadable Specification Sheets: There are no downloadable PDF spec sheets
        for any equipment model. For a DOT engineer building an RFP specification or a sole-
        source justification, a downloadable spec sheet is not optional - it is a fundamental
        requirement. The absence of spec sheets forces the engineer to contact sales for basic
        information that should be self-service.
    •   No Procurement Pathway: There is no clear path from ‘I need this equipment’ to ‘How
        do I procure this through my state contract vehicle?’ The Cooperative Purchasing page
        exists but is invisible from the equipment journey. This is a critical disconnect because
        cooperative purchasing contracts can compress a 6-month procurement timeline to 2
        weeks.
    •   DOTs Market Page is Thin: The Markets > DOTs page exists but provides general
        messaging without the technical depth DOT engineers require. It does not list the
        specific DOT clients ICC-IMS has served, the standards ICC-IMS equipment is certified
        to, or the federal programs (FHWA LTBP, MAP-21/FAST Act) that ICC-IMS equipment
        supports.


Stage 3: Validation and Decision
Current Experience: The DOT engineer wants to validate ICC-IMS’s credibility through DOT-
specific references and case studies. They navigate to Projects and find case studies from
Arizona DOT, Wyoming DOT, Tennessee DOT, and Delaware DOT - but these are mixed into a
geographic gallery alongside municipal projects. There is no way to filter for ‘DOT projects only’
or ‘equipment sales.’

    •   Testimonial Attribution: The WYDOT testimonial on the homepage is compelling but it
        rotates in a carousel with municipal testimonials and is not attributed by segment.
        Placing it prominently on the DOT market page and equipment pages would significantly
        strengthen credibility.
    •   No Reference Device Context: The SurPRO reference device page does not

4.2 Persona B - Municipal/County Public Works Director Journey
Stage 1: Awareness and Entry
Mindset: The municipal buyer is typically reacting to a council directive, an audit finding, a
constituent complaint about road conditions, or an upcoming budget cycle. They may be
evaluating whether to start a pavement management program, update an existing one, or find a
new vendor after an unsatisfying previous experience.

Typical Entry: Google search for ‘pavement condition survey city,’ ‘pavement management
plan consultant,’ ‘sidewalk ADA compliance survey,’ ‘StreetSaver implementation,’ or ‘pavement
management software for municipalities.’ They may also arrive via a Sourcewell or cooperative
purchasing directory, or through a referral from a neighboring city.

Current Experience: The homepage is partially relevant: ‘pavement management’ is in the
headline, and testimonials from cities like Pico Rivera, Dunwoody, and Springfield are relatable.
However, the homepage does not immediately address the top municipal concern: ‘How do I get
budget for pavement work and explain the situation to my council?’

  Friction Point
  The municipal buyer’s core problem is not technical - it is political and financial. They need to

        to serve.
    •   Software Product Confusion: The ESA, ESWA, Unify (Drive, Connect, Inform), and

        buyer visiting the software section cannot quickly understand: ‘What will I get? What will

    •   No Sample Deliverables: There is no sample pavement management report, no

        budget scenario output. Municipal buyers want to see what they will get before they

        confidence to decision makers without giving competitors a peak behind the curtain.

manager and navigate procurement. The Cooperative Purchasing page exists but is
disconnected from the municipal journey. There is no ROI framing, no language about typical

4.3 Persona C - AEC Firm Project Manager Journey
Stage 1: Awareness and Entry
Mindset: The AEC project manager is searching for a reliable subcontractor for a specific
project scope. They need to evaluate ICC-IMS quickly: Can they do the work? Will the data
integrate with our systems? Can they meet our schedule? Do they have the insurance,
certifications, and QC documentation we need?

Typical Entry: Referral from a colleague, pre-qualification list search, or Google search for
‘pavement data collection subcontractor,’ ‘ASTM E950 data collection service,’ ‘airfield
pavement assessment service,’ or ‘IrisPRO Pave data collection service.’

Current Experience: There is a Markets > Civil Engineering Firms page, but it is thin and
generic. It describes challenges facing AEC firms without demonstrating how ICC-IMS
specifically solves them. The Services dropdown does list Civil Engineering / Construction Firms
services (12 items), but they overlap significantly with services listed for other segments.


  Friction Point

  The AEC buyer is looking for a partner, not a vendor. Their evaluation criteria are entirely different
  from a government buyer’s: they need to know about data format flexibility, PMS platform integration,
  QC documentation standards, turnaround time commitments, insurance and bonding, and
  subcontractor pre-qualification. None of this information exists on the current site. The AEC buyer

Stage 2: Consideration and Technical Evaluation
    •   No Integration Information: There is no reference to data format outputs or

    •   No AEC Case Studies: The Projects section contains no AEC-focused case studies nor
        testimonials. All projects are presented as direct-to-government engagements, which
        inadvertently signals that ICC-IMS does not work as a subcontractor or does not value

Stage 3: Decision and Pre-Qualification
Current Experience: The AEC project manager needs to add ICC-IMS to a pre-qualified
vendor list or issue a subcontract. There is no downloadable capability statement, no standard

Us’ pathway differentiated for AEC firms versus government agencies.

    •   Critical Gap: A downloadable one-page capability statement formatted for subcontractor
        pre-qualification packages is a standard expectation in the AEC world. Its absence is a

8. GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) Recommendations
Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) refers to practices that increase the likelihood of a
company’s website content being cited, summarized, or referenced by AI search tools including
Google AI Overviews, Bing Copilot, Perplexity, ChatGPT search, Claude, and similar platforms.
For B2B technical companies like ICC-IMS, GEO is becoming as important as traditional SEO
because government agency buyers and AEC professionals are increasingly using AI tools to
research vendors, compare solutions, and evaluate technical specifications.

8.1 Why GEO Matters for ICC-IMS
When a DOT engineer asks an AI tool ‘What companies manufacture ASTM E950-compliant
pavement profilers?’ or a municipal public works director asks ‘Who offers cooperative
purchasing contracts for pavement condition surveys?’ or an AEC project manager asks ‘What
are the best pavement data collection subcontractors in the Southeast?’, the AI tool synthesizes
information from credible, well-structured web pages with clear entity data and factual authority
signals. ICC-IMS should be in every one of those answers. Currently, the site’s content depth,
structural organization, and lack of schema markup make it unlikely to be prominently cited in
AI-generated responses.

8.2 GEO-Specific Recommendations
    •   Answer-First Content Structure: Rewrite key service and equipment pages so that the
        most important factual information appears in the first 100–150 words in clear,
        declarative sentences. AI tools extract and cite content from the opening paragraphs of
        well-structured pages. Example: The IrisPRO Every Speed page should open with ‘The
        IrisPRO Every Speed (ESP) is a patented road profiling system manufactured by ICC-
        IMS that collects accurate IRI and longitudinal profile at any vehicle speed, including
        stop-and-go conditions. Certified to ASTM E950, AASHTO R56, AASHTO R57, and
        TxDOT specifications.’
    •   FAQ Sections on Every Major Page: Add FAQ sections to every service, equipment,
        and market page, addressing the specific questions each persona would ask. FAQs with
        FAQPage schema are among the most frequently cited content types in AI-generated
        answers. Example questions: ‘Does the IrisPRO High Speed profiler comply with ASTM
        E950?’ / ‘What cooperative purchasing contracts does ICC-IMS have for pavement
        condition surveys?’ / ‘What data formats does ICC-IMS support for integration with third-
        party PMS platforms?’
    •   Structured Data and Schema Markup: Implement FAQPage schema on all FAQ
        sections, Product schema on all equipment pages with specifications as structured
        properties, Service schema on all service pages, and Organization schema site-wide
