#### The problem with finding out later
The expensive failure in field data collection is discovering a problem after the crew has left. A sensor drifting out of tolerance, a missed segment, a route run in the wrong direction, a GPS dropout across a corridor - each of these is a minor fix on the day and a remobilization a week later.
Unify Drive addresses that by moving monitoring into the vehicle in real time. Collection quality is visible while the crew is still on the network and while the fix can be addressed more cost effectively.
#### What Unify Drive does
Route planning. Collection routes built against the network inventory, sequenced for efficient coverage, with progress tracked against the plan. On a large network, the difference between a planned route set and improvised coverage is measured in days.
Real-time collection monitoring. Live visibility into what is being collected and whether it meets parameters, so problems surface during the shift.
Fleet management. Multiple vehicles and crews coordinated across a program - who is collecting what, where, and how much of the network is complete.
#### Who uses it
ICC-IMS crews, across the largest municipal and county collection fleet in the industry. Every capability on this page is proven in daily production use rather than in demonstration.
Agencies and firms operating their own fleets - state DOTs running in-house collection programs and AEC firms with owned collection capability. ### Body sections
#### Why automated QC matters more than you might think
Manual QC of a network dataset is a sampling exercise. A reviewer examines a fraction of the records and infers the rest. On a large network that fraction is small, and the records that most need review are exactly the anomalies a sample is least likely to catch.
Automated validation inverts this. Every record is checked against defined criteria, and human review is directed to what the check flagged rather than to a random subset. The reviewer’s time goes to judgment calls instead of to reading clean records.
#### What Connect checks
Validation against acceptance criteria. Criteria defined for the program - ranges, continuity, positional accuracy, sensor agreement - applied to every record.
Flagging for review. Records outside criteria surfaced for human judgment rather than silently discarded. The distinction matters: an anomaly is sometimes real pavement.
Quality documentation generation. A record of what was checked, what passed, what was flagged, and how flags were resolved.
[CONFIRM - the specific validation checks performed and whether acceptance criteria are configurable per agency. State DOTs and AEC firms with their own QC standards will ask whether Connect can be configured to their criteria or only to defaults.]
#### The QC record as a deliverable
For a state DOT, data supporting federal reporting has to withstand review, and “we checked it” is not a procedure. A generated QC record is.
For an AEC firm, the deliverable to their your carries your company’s name and your seal. A subcontractor who hands over a documented QC record is materially easier to defend to a client than one who simply asserts data quality.
For a municipality, the QC record is what answers a consulting engineer or a skeptical council member asking how the numbers were produced.

### Body sections
#### Data that does not persuade does not matter
A pavement management system tells an agency what to do. It does not get the money approved. That happens in a public meeting, in front of people without technical backgrounds, competing against every other departmental need.
Unify Inform is built for that room.
The measure of its success is not analytical depth - ESA handles that - but whether a council member looks at the screen and immediately understands the situation.
#### What Inform provides
Interactive condition dashboards. Network condition, trends, and program status in a view a non-technical reader can navigate. A map colored by condition communicates in seconds what a table of PCI scores does not communicate at all.
Live query during a meeting. Every public meeting includes someone asking about a specific street. Pulling up that segment’s actual condition data on the spot does more for credibility than the entire prepared presentation - and answering “we’ll follow up” does the opposite.
Public-facing portals. Residents can see the condition of their own street and where it sits in the program. This converts the most common complaint call into self-service, and it makes the prioritization visible, which is generally what residents actually want to know: not why their street is bad, but whether anyone has a plan for it.
Council presentation tools. Visuals and views built for presentation rather than for analysis.
[CONFIRM - full feature set, whether public portals are a standard or premium configuration, and what branding and configuration options agencies have.]
#### Transparency as a strategy
Publishing condition data publicly makes some agencies nervous, and the concern is understandable: it documents, in public, that a substantial share of the network is in poor condition.
Agencies that do it anyway generally find the opposite effect. Publishing the data reframes the conversation from “the city is neglecting my street” to “here is the condition of the whole network, here is the plan, here is what the current budget funds.” It converts an accusation of incompetence into a discussion about resources - which is the discussion the agency wants to be having, and one it cannot start without the data being visible.
It also builds the constituency for a funding measure. Residents who can see the backlog are more likely to support paying to address it.

### Body sections
#### What ESA does, in plain terms
Tells you which roads to fix first. Not the worst ones - the ones where money does the most good. Preserving a road in fair condition costs a fraction of rebuilding it after it fails, which is why worst-first spending produces a network that declines while costs rise.
Shows what your budget actually buys. Model funding levels side by side and see projected network condition under each. The most useful number ESA produces is the annual funding required simply to keep conditions from getting worse.
Projects how long each road has left. Remaining service life by segment, so the program is built on where the network is heading rather than only where it is.
Puts it on a map. GIS-integrated condition mapping, which is how the situation gets communicated to anyone who is not a pavement engineer.
Tracks whether it is working. Year-over-year trends showing whether the program is improving the network - the evidence that determines whether the next funding request succeeds.
#### The technical details
Deterioration modeling - condition forecasting by segment and pavement type, calibrated to observed network performance. Treatment matching and optimization - mapping condition and distress to appropriate treatments at agency unit costs, then optimizing program selection across the network. Budget scenario engine - multi-scenario modeling across funding levels, treatment strategies, and horizons. GIS integration - spatial condition data and mapping. Remaining service life projections - by segment. GASB 34 reporting - supporting the modified approach to infrastructure asset reporting.

### Key facts panel

### Body sections
#### Why sidewalk data needs its own system
Sidewalk management is not pavement management with different assets. Two differences drive it.
Compliance is binary in a way condition is not. A road at PCI 55 is in fair condition. A sidewalk with a cross slope over the limit is non-compliant regardless of how sound the concrete is. The system has to track compliance status independently of condition.
The record is part of the deliverable. An ADA transition plan requires a documented inventory of barriers and a schedule for removing them, and the agency’s position when a complaint arrives depends substantially on being able to produce both. ESWA maintains that record as the work proceeds rather than requiring it to be reconstructed later.
Note: ESWA supports accessibility program management and is not legal advice on ADA obligations. Agencies should confirm their specific obligations with counsel. Recommend a standing disclaimer in the page footer.
#### What ESWA does
Maintains the inventory. Sidewalk segments, curb ramps, and crossings with condition and compliance attributes, mapped.
Tracks compliance status. Which facilities meet criteria, which do not, and on what basis.
Prioritizes remediation. Ranking barriers for a program that has less money than barriers - typically weighting severity, pedestrian demand, and proximity to transit, schools, and public facilities.
Documents the transition plan. Progress against schedule, maintained as an ongoing record.
Reports. For internal management, council reporting, and response to inquiries.
[CONFIRM full feature set, including whether ESWA handles complaint intake and tracking. Complaint intake is a natural fit - complaints are a primary way agencies learn about barriers - and if the capability exists it is a meaningful differentiator.]
#### The liability argument, stated carefully
An agency with a documented inventory, a prioritized remediation plan, and a record of progress is in a substantially different position from one without. The first has evidence of a good-faith program. The second has nothing to show, and the absence of documentation tends to become the story.
What this does not mean is that ESWA prevents claims. Recommend that this page make the distinction explicit rather than implying protection it cannot deliver - overstating it invites exactly the challenge the agency is trying to avoid, and understating it is more credible to the counsel who will review the purchase.
#### Managing sidewalks and pavement together
Agencies running both use ESWA alongside ESA (hyperlink), with data from combined surveys. Contracting sidewalk and pavement surveys together is typically more economical than separately – as an example, the City of Springfield, Missouri holds both.
See Sidewalk & ADA Compliance Surveys (hyperlink).

### FAQ block
What pavement management systems does ICC-IMS support? More than [CONFIRM number] platforms, including StreetSaver, MicroPAVER, PAVER, Cartegraph, Lucity, Brightly, dTIMS, Agile Assets, ArcGIS, and state DOT-specific systems.
What data formats does ICC-IMS export? XML, CSV, SHP, KML/KMZ, PPF, ERD, and PRO. PPF, ERD, and PRO are ProVAL-compatible.
Do we have to switch to ICC-IMS software? No. Data is delivered into your existing platform, with no penalty in data quality or delivery.
Is ICC-IMS profile data compatible with ProVAL? Yes. PPF, ERD, and PRO export formats are all ProVAL-compatible.
Can you deliver data in our client’s format? Yes. This is standard for AEC subcontract work, where the deliverable goes into the end client’s system.
What if our platform is not on the list? Reach out. We can tell you whether we have that capability or what the process would be to integrate with your platform.
