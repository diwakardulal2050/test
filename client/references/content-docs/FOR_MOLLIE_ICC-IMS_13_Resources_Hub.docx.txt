### 3.1 What Is IRI?
Element
Value
URL

Title tag
What Is IRI? International Roughness Index Explained
Meta description
IRI is the standard measure of pavement ride quality, reported in inches per mile or meters per kilometer, calculated from a measured longitudinal profile.
H1
What Is IRI (International Roughness Index)?
Definition. The International Roughness Index (IRI) is the standard measure of pavement ride quality, calculated from a measured longitudinal road profile and reported in inches per mile (in/mi) or meters per kilometer (m/km). Higher IRI values indicate a rougher ride.
How it is calculated. IRI is not measured directly. A profiler measures the elevation profile of the wheel path, and that profile is run through a mathematical simulation of a standard vehicle - the “quarter-car” model - traveling at 80 km/h (50 mph). IRI is the accumulated suspension movement of that simulated vehicle per unit distance.
The consequence is important and often misunderstood: IRI is a property of the road, not of the vehicle that measured it. Two properly certified profilers running the same section at different speeds should produce the same IRI, because the simulation speed is fixed regardless of collection speed.
Typical values.
Condition

IRI (m/km)
New or recently resurfaced pavement

Under 1.0
Good
60–95
1.0–1.5

Thresholds vary by agency and functional class. FHWA applies specific thresholds for federal reporting; individual states set their own for acceptance and programming. [CONFIRM whether to cite current FHWA threshold values, which would strengthen the page considerably but must be current.]
Governing standards. ASTM E1926 covers computing IRI from a longitudinal profile. ASTM E950 covers the profiling equipment. AASHTO R56 covers certification of inertial profiling systems, and AASHTO R57 covers their operation.
Why it matters. IRI is the primary pavement condition metric in federal reporting, it drives smoothness incentive and disincentive payments on paving contracts, and it correlates with what the traveling public actually experiences. Of all pavement metrics, IRI is the one a resident would recognize if they felt it.
A practical limitation worth knowing. Conventional inertial profilers require a minimum speed of roughly 12 mph to produce valid IRI, because below that the accelerometer signal degrades relative to noise. This is why urban and congested sections are frequently missing from IRI datasets. ICC-IMS’s patented IrisPRO Every Speed (hyperlink) collects valid IRI at any speed including a full stop.
Related: PCI · PMS (hyperlink)
FAQ. What does IRI stand for? International Roughness Index. · What is a good IRI value? Below about 95 in/mi is generally considered good; below 60 in/mi is typical of new pavement. · Is a lower IRI better? Yes - lower means smoother. · How is IRI measured? With an inertial profiler that measures the longitudinal elevation profile, from which IRI is computed by simulation. · Is IRI the same as PCI? No. IRI measures ride quality; PCI measures surface distress. A road can have good IRI and poor PCI.
### 3.2 What Is PCI?
Element
Value
URL

Title tag
What Is PCI? Pavement Condition Index Explained
Meta description
PCI is a 0 to 100 rating of pavement surface condition derived from observed distress type, severity, and extent, defined by ASTM D6433 for roads.
H1
What Is PCI (Pavement Condition Index)?
Definition. The Pavement Condition Index (PCI) is a numerical rating of pavement surface condition on a scale of 0 to 100, where 100 represents pavement in perfect condition. PCI is derived from the type, severity, and extent of distresses observed on the pavement surface, and is defined for roads and parking lots by ASTM D6433.
How it is calculated. Distresses are identified and quantified - cracking, rutting, raveling, patching, potholes and others, each classified by severity. Each combination of distress type, severity, and density produces a deduct value, and the deduct values are combined and subtracted from 100.

Category boundaries vary slightly between agencies. Confirm your agency’s own bands before comparing.
Two standards, not one. ASTM D6433 applies to roads and parking lots. ASTM D5340 applies to airfield pavements and uses a distress set specific to them. The two are not interchangeable, and an airfield PCI is not comparable to a roadway PCI.
Why the number is not the whole story. Two roads at PCI 60 can need entirely different work. One with widespread low-severity cracking is a preservation candidate; one with localized structural failure may need reconstruction. PCI ranks the network; the distress data underneath determines the treatment. Any pavement management process that uses the score without the distress data behind it will select the wrong treatments.
PCI versus OCI. Some agencies use an Overall Condition Index (OCI), an agency-defined composite that may weight additional factors such as ride quality or structural condition. OCI definitions vary by agency; PCI under D6433 is standardized. When comparing across agencies, confirm which index is in use.
How it is collected. Traditionally by walking inspectors sampling representative sections. Automated collection using 3D laser scanning now measures distress across the full network rather than a sample - see Pavement Condition Surveys (hyperlink).
Related: IRI · PMS · GASB 34 –(hyperlink all three)
FAQ. What is a good PCI score? Above 85 is generally good; below 55 indicates rehabilitation is needed. · What is the difference between PCI and IRI? PCI measures surface distress; IRI measures ride quality. They are independent - a smooth road can be badly cracked. · Is PCI the same for airfields? No. Airfield PCI follows ASTM D5340 with a different distress set. · How often should PCI be updated? Most agencies survey every two to three years. · Can PCI be measured automatically? Yes, using 3D laser scanning with automated distress classification.
### 3.3 What Is a PMS?
Element
Value
URL
/resources/glossary/pms-pavement-management-system/
Title tag
What Is a Pavement Management System (PMS)?
Meta description
A pavement management system stores road condition data and uses it to recommend which segments to treat, with what treatment, in which year, at what cost.
H1
What Is a Pavement Management System (PMS)?
Definition. A pavement management system (PMS) is software that stores a road network’s inventory and condition data and uses it to recommend which segments to treat, with which treatment, in which year, and at what cost. A PMS turns condition data into a defensible, prioritized capital program.
What a PMS actually does.
Function
What it produces
Inventory
The definitive record of what the agency owns
Condition tracking
Current and historical condition by segment
Deterioration modeling
Forecast condition, by segment and pavement type
Treatment matching
Appropriate treatments for each condition and distress combination
Prioritization
The program that produces the best network outcome for the money
Budget scenario modeling
What different funding levels produce over time
Reporting
Outputs for management, councils, and compliance
The distinction that matters most. A PMS does not simply rank roads worst-first. Pavement deterioration accelerates, and treatment cost rises with it: preserving a road in fair condition costs a fraction of reconstructing one that has failed. A properly configured PMS optimizes across the network, which frequently means treating roads that still look acceptable. That recommendation is counterintuitive to non-engineers and is the main thing a PMS output has to be able to defend.
Common platforms. StreetSaver, MicroPAVER and PAVER, Cartegraph, Lucity, Brightly, dTIMS, Agile Assets, ArcGIS-based systems, agency-developed state DOT systems, and ESA (hyperlink).
Do you need one? Any agency large enough that it cannot decide by inspection - practically, more than a few dozen centerline miles - benefits from a PMS. The more common failure is not the absence of a system but an implemented system nobody maintains, because the data went stale and staff stopped trusting it. A PMS with three-year-old condition data produces confident recommendations about a network that no longer exists.
Related: PCI · IRI · GASB 34 (hyperlink all three)
FAQ. What does PMS stand for? Pavement Management System. · What is the difference between a PMS and a GIS? A GIS maps and stores spatial data; a PMS models deterioration and recommends treatments. Many PMS platforms include GIS. · Do small cities need a PMS? Generally yes, above a few dozen centerline miles. · What data does a PMS need? Network inventory, current condition, treatment options and unit costs. · Can we use our existing PMS with an outside survey provider? Yes - see Integrations & Data Formats (hyperlink).
### 3.4 What Is HFST?
Element
Value
URL
/resources/glossary/hfst-high-friction-surface-treatment/
Title tag
What Is HFST? High Friction Surface Treatment
Meta description
HFST is a surface treatment of high-hardness aggregate in a polymer binder, applied at curves and ramps to restore friction and reduce wet-weather crashes.
H1
What Is HFST (High Friction Surface Treatment)?
Definition. High Friction Surface Treatment (HFST) is a thin pavement surface treatment consisting of a high-hardness, polish-resistant aggregate - most commonly calcined bauxite - bonded to the existing pavement with a polymer resin binder. It is applied at specific, targeted locations to restore or increase surface friction and reduce wet-weather crashes.
Where it is used. HFST is a spot treatment, not a network treatment. It is applied where friction demand is highest and crash history documents a problem: horizontal curves, freeway ramps and loops, intersection and signal approaches, steep downgrades, and bridge decks.
Why calcined bauxite. Conventional aggregates polish under traffic, losing microtexture and therefore friction, exactly where vehicles are braking or cornering hardest. Calcined bauxite resists polishing, so the friction improvement persists rather than decaying within a season or two.
Why it is cost-effective despite being expensive. HFST costs substantially more per lane-mile than conventional surfacing, but it is applied only where the crash reduction benefit is concentrated, a few hundred feet of curve rather than an entire corridor. That targeted application is what makes the economics work. The Federal Highway Administration estimates that HFST reduces wet-weather crashes by 83 percent and total crashes by 57 percent, a scale of improvement conventional resurfacing does not deliver. When the treatment is limited to the curves and approaches where crash risk is highest, the cost per crash prevented compares favorably to corridor-wide paving projects, even though the material cost per lane-mile is higher.
Acceptance testing. Because HFST is installed specifically to deliver friction, verifying that the installed treatment meets its specified friction level is the point rather than a formality. Acceptance testing is normally performed with a locked-wheel friction tester under ASTM E274. See Friction Testing Services (hyperlink) and the SFT Locked Wheel Friction Tester (hyperlink).
Related: Friction testing · PCI (hyperlink both)
FAQ. What does HFST stand for? High Friction Surface Treatment. · What aggregate is used? Most commonly calcined bauxite, chosen for polish resistance. · Where is HFST applied? Curves, ramps, intersection approaches, downgrades, and bridge decks with wet-weather crash history. · How long does HFST last? Service life varies with traffic and installation quality; agencies typically monitor with periodic friction testing. · How is HFST tested after installation? Acceptance testing with a locked-wheel friction tester under ASTM E274.
### 3.5 What Is PROWAG?
Element
Value
URL
/resources/glossary/prowag-accessibility-guidelines/
Title tag
What Is PROWAG? Public Right-of-Way Accessibility
Meta description
PROWAG sets accessibility criteria for sidewalks, curb ramps, and crossings in the public right-of-way, covering slope, width, and surface requirements.
H1
What Is PROWAG (Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines)?
Definition. PROWAG refers to the Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines, developed by the United States Access Board to set accessibility criteria for pedestrian facilities in the public right-of-way - sidewalks, curb ramps, crosswalks, pedestrian signals, and street furniture. PROWAG addresses the right-of-way environment specifically, where earlier accessibility standards focused on buildings and sites.
What it covers. Pedestrian access routes, including width and surface requirements; running slope and cross slope limits; curb ramps and blended transitions; detectable warning surfaces; accessible pedestrian signals; and transit stops.
The criterion that catches agencies out. Cross slope - the slope across the direction of travel - is limited to a maximum that many existing sidewalks exceed, frequently at driveway crossings where the sidewalk is warped to meet the drive apron. Driveway crossings are the single most common source of non-compliance in an inventory, and a sidewalk in excellent structural condition can be entirely non-compliant on cross slope alone. This is why an accessibility survey has to measure geometry, not just assess condition.

PROWAG and the ADA. The ADA establishes the legal obligation; PROWAG provides the technical criteria for pedestrian facilities in the right-of-way. Agencies generally document compliance through an ADA transition plan: an inventory of existing barriers, a schedule for removing them, and a record of progress.

### 3.6 What Is an FWD?
Element
Value
URL
/resources/glossary/fwd-falling-weight-deflectometer/
Title tag
What Is an FWD? Falling Weight Deflectometer
Meta description
A Falling Weight Deflectometer measures pavement structural capacity by dropping a known load and recording the resulting pattern of deflections.
H1
What Is an FWD (Falling Weight Deflectometer)?
Definition. A Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) is a device that measures the structural capacity of a pavement by dropping a known weight onto the surface and recording the resulting deflection at several distances from the load. The pattern of deflections - the deflection basin - is used to back-calculate the stiffness of each pavement layer and the subgrade beneath.
Surface condition versus structural capacity. This is the distinction the page exists to make, and it is one that costs agencies real money when it is missed.
Surface condition - PCI and IRI (hyperlink both) - describes what the pavement looks like and rides like. Structural capacity describes how much load the pavement can carry before it fails. They are independent. A road can look acceptable and be structurally exhausted, in which case a surface treatment is money spent on a pavement that will fail underneath it within a year or two. The reverse also occurs: a structurally sound pavement with poor surface condition may need nothing more than a surface treatment.
How it works. The FWD is trailer-mounted and stationary during testing. A weight is dropped onto a loading plate to apply an impulse simulating a passing wheel load, and geophones at increasing distances record deflection. Testing is done at discrete points along the segment.
When it is used. Project-level design, before rehabilitation, to determine what the pavement can carry and what treatment is warranted. Also for load rating, forensic investigation of premature failure, and validating network-level assumptions.
FWD, LWD, and TSDD. A Light Weight Deflectometer (LWD) is a portable version for spot testing and construction quality control. A Traffic Speed Deflection Device (TSDD) measures deflection continuously at traffic speed, enabling network-level structural screening without lane closures - historically impossible, because FWD testing is stationary and requires traffic control.

### 3.7 What Is GASB 34?
Element
Value
URL
/resources/glossary/gasb-34/
Title tag
What Is GASB 34? Infrastructure Reporting Explained
Meta description
GASB 34 requires governments to report infrastructure assets, including roads, and allows a modified approach based on documented condition assessment.
H1
What Is GASB 34 and How Does It Affect Pavement Reporting?
Definition. GASB Statement No. 34 is the Governmental Accounting Standards Board standard that requires state and local governments to report capital assets - including infrastructure such as roads, bridges, and sidewalks - in their annual financial statements. It is the reason pavement condition data appears in a finance department’s requirements as well as a public works department’s.
Two ways to report infrastructure.
Approach
How it works
What it requires
Depreciation
Infrastructure is capitalized and depreciated over its useful life
Standard asset accounting
Modified approach
Infrastructure is not depreciated, provided the government documents that it is being preserved at or above an established condition level
An asset management system, condition assessments at least every three years, and documented estimates of the cost to maintain the condition level
Why the modified approach matters to public works. Under the modified approach, maintenance and preservation spending is expensed rather than depreciated, and the agency avoids reporting accumulating depreciation on its road network. To use it, the government must have an asset management system, perform condition assessments at least every three years, and document both the established condition level and the annual cost to maintain it.
The practical effect is that GASB 34 gives a public works director an ally in the finance department. Condition surveys and a pavement management system stop being a public works preference and become a documented requirement of the reporting approach the government has adopted. A director struggling to fund a survey program is often one conversation with the finance director away from a different answer.
The three-year interval. The requirement for condition assessment at least every three years is why many agencies settle on a two- or three-year survey cycle. See Ongoing Monitoring. (hyperlink)
This page is a plain-English explanation of the standard’s practical effect on pavement management, not accounting advice. Agencies should confirm reporting requirements with their finance department and auditors.
